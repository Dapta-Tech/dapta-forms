This guide is for you if you already run ads, you already know the hook matters, and you still can't figure out why your CTR won't take off and your cost per lead stays high. The answer is almost always in the first few seconds. More precisely: in the missing number.

Most hooks don't fail because they're boring. They fail because they're vague. They talk to everyone and hurt no one. "Is your team losing leads?" Maybe, maybe not. A hook like "Out of every 10 leads you get, [N] never hear from you a second time," with your real number, forces the reader to do the math in their head.

<!-- anim: hook-de-dolor/vago-vs-numero | A feed scrolls fast past a vague hook and stops dead on the hook with a concrete number of lost leads -->

A quantified pain hook has three parts that never come apart:

1. **A pain your customer already feels**, not one you assume they should feel.
2. **A number** that turns that pain from a vague feeling into a concrete loss in money, time or percentage.
3. **An implied cause** that points to where the problem comes from and opens the door to your solution.

If you apply just one thing this week, make it the 4-part formula. Everything else builds toward it.

## Why pain stops the scroll

In 1979, Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky published prospect theory: people feel a loss roughly twice as strongly as a gain of the same size. They called it loss aversion.

In your feed, that means "Learn how to make more this year" competes with every promise your audience has already heard and no longer believes. "You're letting [amount] slip away every month without knowing it" feels like a threat to something they already own. This doesn't mean scary hooks. It means the reader feels, down to the number, that they're already losing something.

## The 3 types of pain

Before you write, figure out how aware your audience is of their problem:

| Type | Who they are | Audience and conversion | How it sounds |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aware | Knows they have the problem and googles it | Small, converts high | "If you have more leads than you can reach, you're paying for deals you'll never close." |
| Latent | Sees the symptom, doesn't connect it to the cause | Large, converts medium | "If your ad spend grows faster than your sales, the problem probably isn't your ads." |
| In denial | Has the problem and downplays it | Very large, converts low at first | Needs the most precise number of the three to break the "it's not that bad." |

## Pain map in 15 minutes

1. **3 minutes:** write the symptom in the exact words your customer would use with a colleague.
2. **3 minutes:** what event makes them look for a solution today and not in 6 months?
3. **5 minutes:** calculate the cost. Illustrative example: 5 reps × 45 minutes a day on manual follow-up × $25 an hour = about $94 a day, over $2,000 a month.
4. **4 minutes:** write the hook with that number. Number first.

A formatting detail: "2 hours a day" hurts more than "730 hours a year," and "[amount] a month" hurts more than the yearly figure, because the month is the unit decision makers think in.

## Where to find the exact number

"47 minutes" reads as data. "Almost an hour" reads as opinion. These are your sources, from most powerful to easiest:

1. **Your own customers.** The most powerful and the least used. Ask them how long things took before, how many leads they lost and what it cost them (the form is below).
2. **Your campaign and funnel metrics:** leads who don't finish signing up, time from lead to first contact, demo show rates. Once a month, look for the anomaly that hurts.
3. **Sales call transcripts.** Search for "I figured," "we're losing," "it takes us."
4. **Reviews of 3 stars or less** on G2, Capterra or Google, in your category and your competitors'.
5. **Reddit, Facebook groups and industry forums.** People describe their problems there with an honesty you'll never get in a survey.
6. **Industry studies** and annual reports from the big players in your category.
7. **Comparison sites and your competitors' reports.**

**How to cite it without sounding made up:** number plus context on where it comes from or who it applies to ("based on a survey of our customers," "on teams of 3 to 8 agents"). Never make up a number: if a buyer catches it, you've lost their trust in the whole ad. If you don't have the exact figure, use a range ("4 to 6 out of 10"): a range reads as honest.

## The 4-part formula

In this exact order:

1. **Specific number.** It goes first because it's what stops the scroll.
2. **Consequence.** The translation into a loss the reader can calculate for their own business.
3. **Diagnosis.** The cause lives in the system, never in the person.
4. **Way out.** The promise that a solution exists, without revealing it yet.

Illustrative example with all 4 parts (swap in your own number): "Out of every 10 leads, [6] never get a second touch. With your deal size and close rate, that's money you already paid to generate. Your reps aren't bad: manual follow-up just doesn't scale. There's a way for every lead to get a reply in minutes, without hiring anyone."

<!-- anim: hook-de-dolor/formula-de-4-partes | The 4 parts of the hook come together in order: number, consequence, diagnosis and way out; without the number, the hook falls apart -->

| If it's missing... | This happens |
|---|---|
| The number | The hook is an opinion and the reader keeps scrolling |
| The consequence | The number floats in a vacuum and never hits their own bottom line |
| The diagnosis | The reader feels attacked and gets defensive |
| The way out | It ends in disaster and the brain learns to avoid you |

**By channel:** on cold Meta traffic, use latent or in-denial pain with an everyday number. On Google Search, use aware pain with a more technical number. On skippable video, put the most surprising number in the first 5 seconds.

**The 5 mistakes:** the number at the end, "businesses" instead of a specific customer ("insurance agencies with 3 to 8 agents"), a diagnosis that blames, a hard sell in the way out when the audience is cold, and a hook that doesn't fit in 4 to 6 sentences.

## 8 fill-in templates

1. **The number that hurts:** "[%] of [audience] are [action] without knowing it. At [deal size] and a [rate]% close rate, that's [amount] a month in [loss]. [Cause]. [Promise]."
2. **The comparison:** "[Type A businesses] hit [metric] in [better time]. [Type B] take [worse time]. In [industry], that gap means [consequence]."
3. **The anonymous case:** "A [type of business] in [city] was getting [N] leads a month. [X]% never got [action]. Once they changed that, [result] in [time]." Only with a case you actually have.
4. **The hidden cost:** "Did you know [activity that looks free] costs you [amount] a [period]? [Breakdown of the math]."
5. **The expectation gap:** "You think [assumption]. The reality: [data that contradicts it]."
6. **The speed play:** "[N]% of sales go to whoever [action] first. Your competitor who replies in [time] already took [N] of your leads this month."
7. **The diagnosis as a question:** "Why does [business A] close [N]% and [business B] only [N]%? It's not the product or the price: it's [N] processes. The first one is [process]."
8. **The running total:** "Last year you spent [amount] on [activity] to generate [N] leads. [X]% never got more than [N] attempts."

## The 3-hook test with about $300

1. **3 different structures,** not minor variations: for example, templates 1, 2 and 5.
2. **1 campaign, 3 ad sets:** same audience, same placement, same budget (about $15 a day each for 7 days).
3. **Don't touch anything for 7 days.** Stepping in before each ad set gathers enough data is the most common mistake.
4. **Same placements for all three,** so they compete for the same inventory.

A starting point for deciding: if one hook has a clearly lower cost per lead than the other two (around 30%), it's the winner and you double its budget. If two end up very close, run 7 more days. If none hits your target, the problem is your page or your offer, not the hook.

**To scale without burning it out,** change the form, not the substance: the same data in another format ("60%" becomes "6 out of 10"), a different audience in the copy, the same copy as the first frame of a video, a different diagnosis or a different way out.

## Do it with dForms

The most believable number for your hook is the one your own customers give you. Ask for it with a 3-question form they can answer in a minute.

**The form: “3 questions for your customers”.**

1. **Forms → Create form**, layout **Slides**, with **Start from scratch**. Title: "Give us 1 minute."
2. Three **Slider** questions, all about their situation before working with you:
   - Before, how many hours a week did [the task you solve] eat up? (0 to 40)
   - Out of every 10 [leads, customers, orders], how many slipped away? (0 to 10)
   - What do you figure that cost you a month? (ranges in dollars)
3. One optional open question: "How would you put it in your own words?" That's where the symptom for your pain map comes from.
4. **Publish** and send the link by text or email to your last 20 customers.
5. Once you have 10 or more answers, go to **Submissions** and click **Download CSV**. Get the average and the range for each question.

That's part 1 of your formula, with a source: "based on a survey of [N] of our customers." And once the ad is running, make the first question on your landing page form repeat the number from the hook. Whoever clicks lands on the same promise that stopped them.
